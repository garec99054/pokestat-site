What the wider Pokemon card and sealed market did across 31 monthly snapshots, from March 2024 to September 2026. The index charts follow the same fixed group of cards every month, so a rising line means those prices rose, not that more cards were added underneath it. The second half of the page is a separate sealed study: whether sealed boxes and single cards move ahead of one another, and how much of a box price the cards inside it explain. This is one stretch of a rising market, so read it as what happened rather than as what happens next.

Chase-single price index by tier

Each tier is a fixed basket of cards priced in every month, so the line isolates price movement from the changing set of cards printed over time.

A fixed basket of 252 modeled chase singles is essentially flat over the 31 months (index 132.5). Across tiers, SIR is the strongest at 122.9 while Hyper sits at 81.3: mature chase singles have not broadly rallied, and the drift the model rewards is a within-lifecycle set-age effect, not a market-wide bull run.

Model skill versus baselines

How much cross-sectional ranking skill each model shows on the selection folds, against a zero, a momentum, and a reversion baseline.

The selected rf reaches mean IC 0.333 with a +17.4% quintile spread, but the momentum (-0.065) and reversion (+0.150) baselines land near zero -- and the models page shows a non-static edge survives ablating the static set-age features (the basis for the strong verdict): ablation removes a substantial minority of the all-fold signal (22%). The ranking is still substantially a lifecycle ordering — set age alone, with no model, scores 0.309 of the 0.333 full-feature IC.

Do recent winners keep winning?

Sorting the universe into quintiles by past return and holding forward three months, for four different formation windows.

Short formation windows lean toward reversion in point estimate (Q5 recent winners below Q1 losers: rank correlation -0.1 at 1 month, -0.6 at 3 months), but once the overlapping holds are deflated none of the horizons is statistically distinguishable from noise -- no tradeable momentum or reversion. That is the pooled sort, which partly ranks cards on era membership. Ranking the same cards inside their own era instead turns form1m, form3m directional toward reversion; it is a post-hoc, cost-free subgroup result on one calendar window, and the momentum page carries the full comparison and its caveats.

Sealed product versus singles

Booster boxes go out of print and get scarcer; open singles do not. This overlays the two fixed-basket indices on one rebased scale.

Sealed booster boxes have run to index 329.2 while modeled singles held near 132.5. The mechanical scarcity drift as sets go out of print lives in sealed product, not in the singles this site models: an honest reminder that a rising sealed market does not lift the graded-single chase cards with it.

The sealed side of the catalogue

Sealed product is tracked separately from the modeled singles and is never used as a training feature. This block is a standalone study: what sealed data exists, whether sealed and singles prices lead each other, and how a box price compares to the expected value of the hits inside it.

69 sets and 210 sealed products carry a monthly price across 2024-03 to 2026-09; 42 of them clear the gates for the lead/lag study (12+ overlapping months and 5+ chase singles priced throughout); 29 have a computable box EV. The limit to hold in mind throughout: 82.6% of sets have a median of one priced sealed product per month. A set's “sealed price” is usually ONE product's series, not a cross-sectional average, so a single delisting or one thin-volume seller moves it and there is no within-set averaging to damp that.

Do sealed prices lead the singles, or just track them?

A booster box is a claim on the singles it contains, so the two prices ought to be linked. This measures the link directly: k < 0 means the singles moved earlier, k > 0 means the sealed price did, k = 0 is contemporaneous.

Neither side leads. The data cannot even establish that they reliably co-move. Not one lag in any of the 4 profiles clears both error bars, so the study claims no direction: every profile’s verdict is insufficient. What the numbers do show descriptively is a symmetric hump centred on zero: the correlation peaks at lag 0 in 4 of 4 profiles, which is the shape of two prices tracking each other rather than one moving first. The size is the interesting part — the contemporaneous correlation runs only 0.133–0.250. A booster box is a claim on the singles inside it, so the naive expectation is near-lockstep co-movement; what is actually there is a weak positive association the conservative error bar cannot separate from the common market tide.

Under the permissive bar alone the reading would be tracks — never leads. Full lag tables are in sealed_summary.md.

How much of a box price do the hits explain?

For every set with both pull-rate data and a booster-box price series, the expected value of the tracked hit tiers is computed per pack, scaled to a 36-pack box, and divided by the box's market price — both sides read at the same month, from historical prices only.

The two lines agree this run. Across all available sets the median ratio ran 11.8% → 11.4% (18 → 29 sets); on the constant basket of 17 sets priced in all 31 months it ran 11.9% → 10.5%. The all-sets line still GROWS as sets enter the panel, so it carries a composition effect the constant basket does not -- but on this panel that effect is not large enough for the study to call the two readings different, so no divergence is claimed here.

What this EV number ignores

This is a floor on box value, not a valuation. The study's own list:

  • Only the tiers pull_rates covers contribute. Commons, uncommons, ordinary rares, reverse holos and energy carry real bulk value that is counted as zero here.
  • Within a tier every card is assumed equally likely, and the tier's expected value is the MEAN market price of its priced cards. Real pack collation is not uniform.
  • Prices are TCGplayer raw near-mint market prices. Condition, centering, and any grading upside or downside are ignored.
  • Seller fees, shipping, and sales tax are ignored on both sides.
  • A box is assumed to hold 36 packs (see PACKS_PER_BOX). The ratio is exactly linear in that count.
  • packs_per_hit is a point estimate from a finite opening study (sample_size packs); its own sampling error is not propagated.
  • Guaranteed-hit collation inside a box (a box is not 36 independent packs) is ignored; only the average rate is used.
  • Sealed product has option value -- it can be held, opened later, or graded as sealed. None of that is priced here.

What the sealed study deliberately does not do

Lifted from the study itself, so these travel with every number above.

  • Sealed is NOT wired into the forward-model training panel. It is a set-level cross-section with its own leakage questions (a sealed price is shared by every card in the set, so a naive card-level merge manufactures cross-sectional dependence). The panel's existing sealed_trend_3m feature is unchanged by this study.
  • No predictive claim. Nothing here is scored out-of-sample against future returns. The lead/lag profiles are contemporaneous descriptions of a co-movement structure, not a trading signal.
  • 82.6% of sets have a median of one priced sealed product per month. A single delisting, relisting, or one thin-volume seller moves that set's entire "sealed price" for the month, and there is no within-set averaging to damp it.
  • Booster-bundle prices are discarded upstream by sealed_index, and ETB prices only stand in where a set never had a booster box. Mixing pack counts would corrupt the EV ratio, so the EV section uses booster boxes only.
  • Pull rates are a single snapshot per set from one opening study, applied to every month. Collation does not actually change over a set's life, so this is benign, but the rate's own sampling error is not propagated.
